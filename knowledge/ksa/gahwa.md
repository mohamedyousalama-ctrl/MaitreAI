# القهوة العربية (Gahwa)

> **This entry is the template for the pack.** Every other entry follows this section order.

## Region
Universal across the Kingdom — the single most important hospitality symbol in Saudi
culture, from Najd to the Hijaz, Asir and the Eastern Province. Regional roast and spicing
differ (below), but *serving gahwa* is common ground everywhere.

## Ingredients
Lightly-roasted Arabica beans (often pale, "golden" roast in Najd), ground and brewed with
**cardamom (هيل)** as the defining spice; frequently **saffron (زعفران)**, sometimes cloves
(قرنفل), ginger (زنجبيل), or a touch of rosewater. Served **without sugar** — the sweetness
comes from the dates eaten alongside.

## Taste
Light-bodied, aromatic, more like a spiced herbal infusion than a dark espresso. Golden to
amber in colour, fragrant with cardamom and saffron, gently bitter, warming. Not the
heavy, dark "Turkish" style — Saudi gahwa is deliberately delicate.

## Cultural significance
Gahwa is **karam** (generosity) made liquid. Offering it is the first act of welcome; refusing
to serve it to a guest is unthinkable. It anchors the *majlis* (sitting room), marks weddings,
funerals, Eid, business, and reconciliation. UNESCO lists Arabic coffee as intangible cultural
heritage. To a Saudi, "قهوة وتمر" (coffee and dates) *is* hospitality.

## Rituals
Poured by the host (or the youngest, as a sign of respect) from a **dallah** (long-spouted
pot) into small handleless cups (**فناجيل**), filled **only a third** — a full cup is
almost rude, because refilling is the point (it means "stay, you're welcome"). The server
holds the dallah in the left hand, cups in the right, and keeps refilling until the guest
**gently shakes the cup side to side** — the signal for "enough, thank you."

## Etiquette
- Take the cup with the **right hand**.
- An odd number of cups (one or three) is traditional; three is generous.
- **Never** set an empty cup down expecting the host to notice — shake it to signal you're done.
- The host offers again and again — **multiple-offer etiquette** is warmth, not pestering.
- Dates are offered with it; declining the first offer and accepting the second is a normal courtesy dance.

## Anecdote
In a Najdi majlis the guest who lets his cup be refilled a fourth time is teased that he's
"family now." An old line: «ما تقوم إلا وأنت شبعان قهوة» — you don't leave until you've had
your fill of coffee.

## Regional variations
- **Najd:** palest roast, heavy cardamom, saffron-forward — the "golden" gahwa.
- **Hijaz:** sometimes a slightly darker roast, more cloves/ginger; the term **قهوة حجازية** exists.
- **Asir:** may lean toward local roasting and ginger warmth.
- **Eastern Province:** cardamom-forward, close to the Gulf style; often served with dates from Al-Ahsa.

## Pairings
**Dates (تمر)** first and foremost. Then date-filled cookies **kleija (كليجا)** and
**maamoul (معمول)** at Eid. In modern cafés, gahwa is served with a small plate of dates or
Saudi sweets. *(All pairings are cultural — offer them only when the tenant menu carries them.)*

## Modern context
Every home keeps a thermos of it; specialty "Saudi coffee" cafés have revived heritage roasts;
it's poured constantly through **Ramadan** evenings and at every gathering. On delivery menus it
appears as «قهوة عربية / قهوة سعودية», hot, often sold with a side of dates.
