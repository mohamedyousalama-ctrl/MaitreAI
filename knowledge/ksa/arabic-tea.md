# شاي (Arabic Tea)

## Region
Universal companion to gahwa across the Kingdom, with a beloved **mint (نعناع)** style in the
south/Asir and a **saffron/cardamom** style in Najd; **Adani milk tea** appears in the Hijaz.

## Ingredients
Strong **red tea (شاي أحمر)**, generously **sweetened**, often steeped with **fresh mint**,
**saffron**, or **cardamom**; the Adani/Hijazi style is brewed with **milk** and spices.

## Taste
Sweet, aromatic and warming; mint versions are refreshing, saffron versions floral and golden,
milk versions creamy and spiced. Almost always sweeter than Western tea.

## Cultural significance
The everyday hospitality drink alongside coffee — offered to guests, sipped through long
majlis evenings, and central to **Ramadan** after iftar. Making good sweet tea is its own art.

## Rituals
Served hot in small glasses (**استكانة/كاسات**), poured generously and refilled; mint sprig on
top in the southern style. Offered with sweets and dates.

## Etiquette
Accept a glass as courtesy; the host keeps the pot coming. Sweetness is assumed unless you ask
for it light («خفيف السكر»).

## Recommendation voice
- «تحب شاي بالنعناع ولا زعفران مع الحلا؟» — **only the variants the item lists.**
- «شاي أحمر دافي يمشي مع اللقيمات.» — pair only with sweets the tenant sells.

## Anecdote
In an Asiri sitting, the mint is torn by hand into the pot — the smell of it is half the point
of the tea.

## Regional variations
- **Asir/south:** mint tea (شاي بالنعناع) is beloved.
- **Najd:** saffron/cardamom red tea.
- **Hijaz:** Adani-style milk tea (شاي حليب/عدني) appears.

## Pairings
Saudi sweets, dates, mutabbaq; served alongside or after gahwa. *(Offer only what the tenant sells.)*

## Modern context
On virtually every Saudi café and restaurant menu, hot and often sold by variant (نعناع/زعفران/
حليب); a big Ramadan seller. Milk-tea variants are dairy — relevant for dairy allergies.
